Yes—**a blueprint first, followed by modeling, rigging, and animation checks, is the right approach.** The biggest improvement is to review the character at each stage instead of asking for a finished model in one step.

Your reference has a clear style: **a sculpted clay wizard with rounded forms, a large nose, heavy eyelids, flowing beard clumps, and soft purple-and-gold materials.** Much of its appeal comes from the silhouette and proportions, before the small details.

Without seeing your previous models, I can’t identify their exact problems. But building a character by stacking spheres, cylinders, and cones often produces the assembled look you described. Smooth shading alone cannot fix disconnected shapes or poor proportions.

**Here is the workflow I recommend for your Roblox character:**

| Stage | What to create | Check before continuing |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Character blueprint** | Front, side, and back views at matching scale; face close-up; color palette; separate staff view | Do all views describe the same character? |
| **2. Simple 3D shape** | Gray model with the head, body, robe, hat, and main beard volume | Does it look appealing from every angle without textures? |
| **3. Sculpted form** | Rounded facial transitions, robe folds, broad beard clumps, slightly irregular hat | Does it feel deliberately sculpted rather than assembled? |
| **4. Game-ready mesh** | Clean surface structure, especially around shoulders, elbows, hands, and robe | Can it bend without collapsing or exposing gaps? |
| **5. Rig and skinning** | Skeleton plus controlled influence over the mesh | Test raised arms, bent elbows, head turns, and a walking stride |
| **6. Clay materials** | Soft surface response, subtle texture, restrained gold details | Does it still look good under Roblox lighting? |
| **7. Animation** | Idle, walk, greeting, and spell cast | Are feet planted, weight balanced, and the staff grip believable? |
| **8. Roblox validation** | Imported character and animation test scene | Check scale, movement, clipping, appearance, and performance |

**Plan the rig early, but finish skinning after the mesh structure is stable.** Rigging supplies the skeleton; skinning controls how the surface bends around it. Both matter for natural movement. Roblox supports this workflow through external modeling tools such as Blender. [Roblox rigging and skinning](https://create.roblox.com/docs/art/modeling/rigging)

For this particular wizard, I would make these choices:

- **Build in a relaxed A-pose:** arms slightly away from the body, hands visible, and staff separate. Create the expressive standing pose afterward.
- **Keep the beard in large flowing clumps:** use subtle surface detail for the fine grooves. Hundreds of thin strands would make the character harder to maintain and animate.
- **Give the robe room to move:** test a walking stride early because the long hem can intersect the legs.
- **Keep intentional separate pieces:** hat, beard, eyes, and staff can be separate meshes and still look cohesive. The goal is clean transitions, not necessarily one mesh.
- **Start with one idle animation:** breathing, a small weight shift, a slow blink, and restrained beard movement. This exposes problems before making a full animation set.

The first technical decision is **NPC or player avatar**. For a wizard NPC, I would start with a custom skinned character. If it must use standard avatar features and animations, plan around Roblox’s R15 structure from the beginning. [Roblox character rig types](https://create.roblox.com/docs/art/modeling/rigging#types-of-rigs)
